## ایران و جهان — subject `world`

People, places and the past: Persian culture and the cultures of the world, maps and geography (Iran's and the
world's), countries, and the history of Iran and of the world. One integrated course instead of separate history,
geography and social-studies lessons, so a child meets a place, its people and its past together (Isfahan is a
city on a map, a Safavid capital, and a bazaar where people work today).

**Approach (modern practice):**

- **From near to far, and back.** Each garden widens the circle (me and my home → neighbourhood and city → Iran →
  the world → the world through time), but every unit starts from the child's own life and comes back to it
  (expanding horizons, with place-based learning: the child's own street, city and province are the first sources).
- **Inquiry, not memorising.** Every unit has a compelling question the child can answer by the end («چرا
  یزدی‌ها بادگیر ساختند؟»، «غذای ما از کجا می‌آید؟»), worked out from pictures, maps, objects and short texts
  (inquiry arc of the C3 Framework, NCSS 2013). Facts such as capitals and provinces are practised as games and
  come back in reviews, but they are never the goal of a lesson on their own.
- **Thinking like a geographer:** reading and making maps step by step (a picture of a room from above → a
  neighbourhood map with a legend → the map of Iran → the globe), directions, scale, and asking why things are
  where they are (where people live, what grows there, why).
- **Thinking like a historian** (from Tree; Seixas' historical thinking concepts): putting things in order on a
  timeline, what changed and what stayed the same, causes and consequences, how we know (evidence from objects,
  buildings and writing), and how people at the time saw things. History is told through what people built,
  invented, traded, wrote and ate, and through ordinary people's daily lives, not through kings and battles.
- **Windows and mirrors.** Persian and Iranian cultures are a mirror (the child sees their own life); other cultures
  are windows. Show real children's everyday lives (school, food, games, homes), similarities first, then
  differences, and avoid the "tourist approach" (only costumes and festivals) and stereotypes.
- **Iran is many peoples.** Persians, Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Lurs, Baluch, Arabs, Turkmen, Gilaks, Mazandaranis,
  Armenians, Assyrians and others, with their languages, music, crafts and foods, are all part of Iran's culture and
  history (`method.html#language`). No ethnic group is shown as more Iranian than another.
- **Modern settings.** Today's Iran and today's world appear next to the past: metro and railways, a science park,
  a nature reserve, a village school, a port. Virtual field trips (animated tours) to UNESCO World Heritage
  sites in Iran and around the world.
- **Off the screen:** interviewing grandparents (oral history), drawing a map of the street, cooking a regional
  dish, visiting a museum or an old house, finding Iran on a globe.

**Neutrality and safety** (`policies.html`), stricter here than anywhere else:

- **Political history of Iran goes up to the Constitutional Revolution** (مشروطه، ۱۲۸۵): what a constitution and a
  parliament are, and that people asked for law. After that, **the 20th century and today are told without
  politics**: daily life, science, technology, cities, the arts and the environment.
  - Periods are named by years («ایران در سال‌های ۱۳۰۰ تا ۱۳۳۰»), never by rulers, dynasties or governments.
  - No leaders and no pictures of leaders; no revolutions, wars, elections or current events after ۱۲۸۵.
  - The test for every sentence: would a family that supports the government and one that opposes it, inside Iran
    and abroad, both read it without objecting? If not, it goes.
  - Seeing an event from several sides is practised on distant history only (how Greeks and Persians described each
    other, how two travellers saw Isfahan), so the skill is learned without today's politics.
  - Family history comes from the family: the child interviews a grandparent about school, games and home; the
    parent note asks to keep it to everyday life.
- **Religion:** no doctrine, no religious holidays and no judgement of any faith. Where history needs it (for example
  how the language and script changed after the 7th century), one short factual sentence, checked by reviewers.
  Places of worship can appear only as architecture (tilework, domes), described as buildings.
- **No glorifying conquest and no battle scenes.** Empires are shown through what they built and how many peoples
  lived and worked together; invasions, if mentioned, through what changed and what was rebuilt (the Maragheh
  observatory after the Mongols).
- **Maps:** borders as on Natural Earth's de facto map; disputed areas are not labelled, and country games use a
  chosen set of countries (Iran's neighbours, then a few per continent), not every country. Names as in Iranian
  schools (خلیج فارس، دریای خزر، دریای عمان).
- **Flags:** every country's flag is shown, except Iran's. Iran's flag is a family's choice: in «بخش والدین» a
  parent can pick the current flag or the lion-and-sun flag (listed side by side, with plain descriptive names and
  no comment), or leave it unset. Lessons never draw Iran's flag themselves; they ask for it (`flag: "iran"`) and get
  the parent's choice. While nothing is chosen, the child sees an empty flag with «پرچم ایران را از پدر و مادرت
  بپرس.» The choice stays on the device like all settings. No anthems of any country.
- **Accuracy:** every lesson is checked by a history or geography teacher before it is published; sources are noted
  in a comment above each lesson. Lessons on the 20th century and today are also checked by two reviewers with
  different views.
- Overlap with other subjects: `science` teaches nature (habitats, animals, climate as science); `world` teaches
  how people live with that nature. Festivals and food are culture here, never religion.

**Expert review still to do:** a history teacher and a geography teacher review this plan (see "Questions for a
history or geography teacher" at the end) and every lesson before it is published; lessons on the 20th century and
today also go to two reviewers with different views.

**Where the systems differ, and what Baghcheh chooses:**

- **Topics or chronology.** Singapore, Finland and Ontario teach by theme in the early years. England teaches history in
  chronological blocks from age 7, and Australia from Year 7. Baghcheh follows both: themes and expanding horizons
  through Sapling, then from Tree a chronological spine of Iran's history next to the world's. Research on
  chronological understanding supports this order: young children handle "before / after" and "long ago" well,
  but not dates and centuries, which are taught from about 9.
- **When economics starts.** Australia starts it in Year 5. C3 and the OECD/INFE framework start at 5–6 with
  earning, choosing and saving. Baghcheh starts small in Sprout, because money habits form early, and becomes
  formal in Tree.
- **Civics.** Rules, cooperation and deciding together come first everywhere, and they are taught from Seed.
  Institutions (parliaments, courts, governments) are taught later in all of these systems. In Baghcheh they are
  taught only in general terms, through history up to ۱۲۸۵, because of the neutrality rules.
- **The 20th century.** Every system above teaches the world wars and 20th-century politics at 12–15 (England
  KS3, Australia Years 9–10). Baghcheh leaves them out. It teaches the 20th century through science, technology,
  daily life and cooperation between countries (see "Questions for a history or geography teacher").
- **How many facts.** England expects every UK county and many world countries. Finland keeps lists short. Baghcheh
  keeps the facts the subject needs (Iran's provinces and their centres, the seas, the neighbours, a chosen set of
  countries per continent) and practises them in generated games, never as the goal of a lesson.

Other school lessons are taught by other subjects: home and street safety (grade 3: 17, 23) and social harms (8: 5)

(6: 13–14) by `health`.

**School column.** «پایه چهارم، درس ۵» means the «مطالعات اجتماعی» textbook of that grade. Iranian schools teach no
«مطالعات اجتماعی» in grades 1–2, and preschool has no textbook, so the Seed and early Sprout objectives say «—».
Baghcheh teaches some topics earlier than school (Iran's map, landforms and provinces in Sprout and Sapling,
following England KS1–KS2), so a «همراه مدرسه» child may meet an objective before its school grade.

**Sizing.** Baghcheh gives one session a week for each hour of «مطالعات اجتماعی» in the school week. Grade 3 has 2
hours, grades 4–5 have about 2.5 (shared with art), and grades 6–9 have 3 (sources: [amoozesh3.ir](https://amoozesh3.ir/), the grade 6 approval reported by
[Mehr](https://www.mehrnews.com/news/1528925), [em90.blogfa.com](https://em90.blogfa.com/post/196) for grades
7–9; check against the official timetable). Before grade 3, where schools have none, the time comes from the
garden's daily budget: Seed 1 session a week, Sprout 1½. New lessons are one session in three up to Sapling and two
in five from Tree (older children take in more per session); the rest is review and the practice generators.

Inquiry (C3 Dimensions 1, 3 and 4: asking, using sources, answering and acting) runs through every strand; each
garden also has an objective for it.

### 🌿 نهال — `sapling`

Iran's land, climates, rivers and provinces; how people live with a dry land; reading maps with grids and scale;
how a town works; goods, services and the bazaar; long ago, from the Stone Age to the first villages; local
history; the continents and Iran's neighbours; World Heritage.

**Sizing:** 2 years × 36 weeks × 2 sessions = 144 sessions: **48 lessons** (all built or being built) and 96 practice and review sessions.
Before: 9 planned lessons, no objectives.

### 🌳 درخت — `tree`

How historians work; the history of Iran from the first cities to Safavid Isfahan next to the world's earliest
civilisations, ancient Greece and the Maya; latitude, longitude, grid references and thematic maps; climate zones
and landforms of the world; Iran's population, water, work, transport, seas and energy; budgets and trade; children's
rights, groups and decisions; countries of the world.

**Sizing:** 3 years × 36 weeks × 2½ sessions = 270 sessions: **108 lessons** and 162 practice and review sessions.
The rows below are the units (one row per unit or main lesson, at least one per objective). Each is split into its
lessons when the garden is planned in detail, before it is built. Before: 13 planned lessons, no objectives.

### Questions for a history or geography teacher

1. **The 20th century in the world.** Strong systems teach the world wars and the 20th century's politics at
   12–15. This plan leaves them out (neutrality, and no war content for children living through one). Should the
   Forest garden name them in one factual sentence each, with no pictures, or leave them out entirely?
2. **Dates in Qajar and Safavid times.** `world-forest-17` gives one factual line on the changes of rulers and on the
   lands lost to Russia. Is that the right level, and how should such losses be worded so that no family objects?
3. **The Cyrus Cylinder and similar objects.** These are ancient, but today they are political symbols. Should
   `world-tree-4` show them as `source` objects like any other, or leave them out?
4. **Religion in history.** The plan allows one factual sentence where history needs it (the 7th century, the
   Safavids). Which sentences, and checked by whom?
5. **Map names and borders.** The plan uses the names taught in Iranian schools and Natural Earth's de facto

6. **Earlier than school.** Iran's map, landforms and provinces come in Sprout and Sapling, two or three years
